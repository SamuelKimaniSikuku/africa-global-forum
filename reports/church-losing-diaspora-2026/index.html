The Church Is Losing the Diaspora.

Religious disaffiliation across Latino, Filipino, Asian and Black communities — a comprehensive analysis of what younger generations are walking away from, and why the same pattern is showing up in every corner of the diaspora at once.

Section 01Executive Summary

Across multiple ethnic and cultural communities — Latino/Hispanic Americans, Filipino migrants, Asian Americans, and African Americans — there is a pronounced and accelerating trend of religious disaffiliation among younger generations. The phenomenon transcends geography, denomination and specific religious tradition, which suggests the driver is structural — sitting inside institutional religion itself — rather than a set of unrelated community-specific problems.

Second- and third-generation members, particularly those with higher education, are abandoning religious institutions at significantly higher rates than their immigrant or first-generation counterparts.

What the data reveals, across communities:

  • Latino Americans — ~49% of US-born Latinos aged 18–29 claim no religious affiliation. This is a generational rupture from the community that has been the demographic backbone of American Catholicism for decades.
  • Filipino migrants — formally renounce their Catholic faith in Germany and Austria to avoid a ~10% income church tax, then in some cases re-embrace Catholicism when they return to the Philippines. Religion tracks context, not conviction.
  • Asian American churches — Korean Presbyterian, Chinese evangelical, Vietnamese and Filipino Catholic congregations are increasingly attended only by Baby Boomers and Gen X. Millennials and Gen Z who grew up in them are almost entirely gone.
  • Black American Gen Z — are leaving the Black Church — the historical spine of Civil Rights — at rates that rival the millennial exodus a decade earlier, driven by hostility to LGBTQ+ members, patriarchal control, judgment culture, prosperity gospel, and perceived inaction on social justice.

The single most consistent complaint across all four communities is the same: church leaders and communities fail to embody the values they preach.

Section 02Latino & Hispanic Americans — The Most Dramatic Shift

Historical context. Latinos have traditionally been the demographic backbone of American Catholicism. For decades the Catholic Church relied heavily on Latin American immigration to maintain — and grow — its congregation numbers in the United States.

Current reality. Four in five Latinos aged 18 to 29 were born in the US, and nearly half (~49%) claim no religious affiliation. Where immigrant parents arrived with deep Catholic commitments, their American-born children are rejecting organised religion outright.

Generational breakdown

  • First-generation immigrants — predominantly Catholic; maintain church attendance and involvement.
  • Second-generation US-born — significant departure from Catholicism; splitting between those claiming no religion and those converting to evangelical Protestantism.
  • Tertiary trend — evangelical conversion rates among Latinos remain minimal and are being overtaken by complete disaffiliation.

Education and gender patterns

The decline is particularly pronounced among educated Latina women. Access to education, critical-thinking skills and economic independence correlate strongly with questioning inherited religious traditions.

The colonialism factor

Younger Latinos increasingly view Catholicism through a decolonial lens — not as an inherited spiritual identity but as a tool of historical oppression, imposed by Spanish colonisation. Interest in pre-Columbian indigenous spiritualities is rising in parallel.

Political note: church decline among Latinos is not uniformly translating into political progressivism. Many second- and third-generation Latinos remain conservative on issues like LGBTQ+ rights and reproductive freedom. Institutional religion is declining, but inherited conservative values persist through family culture rather than religious teaching.

Section 03Filipino Communities — Migration and Loss of Faith

The “abroad effect.” Filipino migrants show a marked pattern: after relocating to other countries, their religious participation and commitment measurably decline. This is not merely reduced attendance — it is a genuine shift in belief and practice.

Concrete evidence from Europe

Some Filipinos in Europe, particularly in Germany and Austria, have abandoned their religion to avoid paying the church tax — Catholics there pay as much as 10% of their income. While economic incentives play a role, the ease with which people formally renounce religion when the option is available suggests the underlying conviction was weaker than it looked.

Generational pattern

  • First-generation abroad — often maintain practice initially; may eventually stop for economic reasons.
  • Second-generation — growing up outside the Philippines with different peer groups and environments, increasingly secular.
  • Return to homeland — some Filipinos who had renounced their faith abroad re-adopt Catholicism upon returning to the Philippines. The practice was culturally embedded, not deeply believed.

Institutional reality

The Philippine Catholic Church is aware of the trend. Germany and Austria formally inform the dioceses in the Philippines when Filipino Catholics there have left the Church; the person’s place of baptism is notified, and the fact is recorded on the baptismal certificate. The bureaucracy of leaving is now itself a well-worn international pathway.

Section 04Asian American Communities — The Dying Congregation

Church decline as a lived community experience. Asian American churches — Korean Presbyterian, Chinese evangelical, Vietnamese Catholic, Filipino Catholic — are undergoing something close to institutional collapse among younger generations.

The age-demographic crisis

Congregations that were vibrant a decade ago now report that the only consistent attendees are Baby Boomers and Gen X. Millennials and Gen Z who grew up in these churches are almost entirely absent, having departed once they reached young adulthood.

Why Asian American youth leave

  • Intellectual suppression — discouragement from asking critical questions about doctrine or religious history.
  • Lack of tangible community support — churches functioned as cultural centres and language-preservation institutions but provided minimal practical help during personal crises, career challenges or relationship difficulties.
  • Hypocrisy observations — youth notice the gap between stated Christian values and actual member behaviour: internal politics, gossip, and judgment of non-conforming members.

Institutional adaptation failure

Unlike progressive churches that have modernised their theology and practices, many traditional Asian American churches have remained rigidly unchanged. That inflexibility appears to accelerate rather than slow departures.

Section 05Black American Gen Z — Exodus from the Traditional Black Church

Historical significance. The Black Church holds a unique place in African American history — as a refuge during slavery and segregation, the institutional home of the Civil Rights Movement, and a cornerstone of Black community life. Its current crisis is therefore a profound shift.

Current state. Gen Z Black Americans are abandoning churches at rates that alarm both pastors and denominational leaders. The trend is being compared to the millennial exodus of the last decade.

Core complaints

1. Hostility to LGBTQ+ community members. Gay and lesbian congregants report feeling fundamentally unwelcome, with pastors explicitly preaching that homosexuality is sinful. Transgender members describe churches as actively hostile. This is particularly damaging for a generation that treats LGBTQ+ acceptance as a basic moral requirement.

2. Patriarchal control and misogyny. Women report extensive restrictions on dress, behaviour and life choices. Sermons focus heavily on women’s duties to husbands and families while men face minimal comparable restrictions. Women are expected to “intercede in prayer” for their husbands as though the men were children requiring maternal guidance. As one Nigerian congregant put it: “There’s no sermon about men not beating their wives, no sermons about men not raping and cheating. Absolutely no standards for men.”

3. Rampant judgment culture. Young women publicly shamed for tank tops, shorts, or other “immodest” clothing. Members’ personal choices around relationships, careers and lifestyle are subject to congregation scrutiny and gossip. The stated purpose of church is undermined by an atmosphere of constant evaluation.

4. Prosperity gospel and financial exploitation. Many churches have adopted “prosperity gospel” theology — the idea that faith produces financial wealth. Pastors collect “love offerings,” demand tithes, and live lavishly while congregations struggle. In one documented case, a congregation learned their pastor had received a private jet as a birthday gift while the church denied financial assistance to members facing hardship. The pattern is viewed as particularly exploitative given the centuries of economic extraction Black communities have already faced.

5. Inaction on social justice. While the historical Black Church drove Civil Rights activism, contemporary churches are seen as politically passive. Despite preaching care for the poor and marginalised, most churches provide minimal community support. Some members live outside the neighbourhoods where their churches operate — attending Sunday service but not investing in the surrounding community.

Section 06Cross-Community Root Causes

Five drivers show up in every community studied.

6.1 The hypocrisy problem

Across Latino, Filipino, Asian and Black communities, the single most consistent complaint is that church leaders and communities fail to embody the values they preach.

  • Priests and pastors preaching sexual morality while concealing or protecting child sexual abuse.
  • Church members preaching Christian love while refusing to support LGBTQ+ family members.
  • Congregations teaching generosity while their leaders accumulate personal wealth.
  • Institutions demanding moral purity from members while maintaining deeply hypocritical internal cultures.

Previous generations rationalised the contradictions or accepted institutional authority. Gen Z has been trained from childhood to question inconsistencies. When the gap between Christian teaching and Christian practice becomes visible, they conclude the entire institution lacks credibility.

6.2 The education effect

There is a direct correlation between higher education and religious disaffiliation across every community studied — college-educated Latinas, Asian Americans at university, Black Americans with college degrees.

Higher education teaches critical evaluation of truth claims, historical context (including how religions were used to justify oppression), exposure to diverse worldviews, and scientific frameworks that conflict with literal religious interpretations. Religious institutions that resist updating their theology — on evolution, geology, LGBTQ+ identity — become increasingly untenable for educated believers.

6.3 The generational assimilation pattern

A consistent pattern emerges across immigrant communities:

  • First (immigrant) — high practice, high belief. Religion is a core part of identity that survived migration because it was genuinely believed.
  • Second (US-born children) — medium-to-high practice, medium belief. Practice is often partly filial duty, partly cultural identity.
  • Third (US-born grandchildren) — low practice, low-to-absent belief. Never experienced the “old country,” see no compelling reason to maintain a practice that doesn’t resonate.

As communities become economically stable and socially integrated, the practical need for religion as a community anchor diminishes.

6.4 The information revolution

Young people today have access to historical documentation of religious institutions’ harms (abuse scandals, complicity in colonialism, support for slavery), alternative communities and support systems outside churches, diverse spiritual and philosophical frameworks, and online spaces where they can connect with others questioning their faith.

The traditional barriers to leaving religion — loss of community, family rejection, social isolation, lack of alternative frameworks — are still there, but substantially lower for young people with internet access and diverse social networks.

6.5 Institutional rigidity

Churches that attempt to modernise and become “welcoming” without fundamentally changing doctrine often fail to attract younger members. Conversely, churches that make genuine theological changes — ordaining women and LGBTQ+ individuals, explicitly rejecting homophobia, engaging in social-justice work — retain younger members better.

Many traditional churches, particularly in immigrant communities, resist these changes precisely to maintain cultural continuity and authority structures. The result is a tragic mismatch: the more they try to preserve tradition, the faster young people leave.

Section 07The Paradox of Cultural Religion

Many respondents describe themselves as “culturally Catholic” or “culturally Christian” without being religiously observant. This category includes people who celebrate religious holidays (Christmas, Easter) without believing the theology; who appreciate religious rituals and aesthetics without accepting doctrine; who want to preserve ethnic/cultural identity without embracing faith.

“I like some of the things that come with it — posadas, the food my mom makes during Lent, and Christmas — but I wouldn’t say I am religious.”

The sustainability question

Can religious institutions survive on cultural practice alone? History suggests not. When religion becomes purely cultural rather than spiritually meaningful:

  • Financial contributions decline — why tithe for a culture?
  • Participation becomes occasional rather than regular.
  • Young people see no reason to pass it on to their own children.
  • Institutions lose their moral authority.

The long-term trajectory for many communities is therefore not merely reduced religiosity but complete institutional decline within two to three generations.

Section 08Why Churches Are Failing to Adapt

The structural bind

Conservative impulse: older generations, who provide most funding and volunteer labour, demand that tradition remain unchanged. Church leadership treats doctrinal consistency as sacred. Modernisation is experienced as betrayal.

Youth reality: younger people will not participate in institutions they find morally objectionable. Inclusion of LGBTQ+ people and feminist theology are non-negotiable for many Gen Z believers. Modern education has made literal scriptural interpretation untenable for many.

The vicious cycle

  • Older members demand unchanging tradition.
  • Young people leave because tradition feels oppressive.
  • Congregation ages and shrinks.
  • Church becomes more financially desperate.
  • Leadership becomes more conservative to retain the remaining members.
  • More young people leave.
  • Cycle repeats until institutional collapse.

What churches that survive share

  • Theologically progressive — acceptance of LGBTQ+ people, feminist theology, engagement with social justice.
  • Intellectually honest — acknowledgment of historical harms, willingness to engage scientific knowledge.
  • Practically engaged — active community service, material support for members, advocacy for marginalised people.
  • Less hierarchical — reduced emphasis on pastoral authority, more democratic decision-making.

Even so, these churches serve a small minority of young people.

Section 09Broader Sociological Implications

What religious decline actually means

The decline documented in these communities is not just about reduced church attendance. Churches historically provided mental-health support and crisis assistance, job networking and economic opportunity, life-milestone rituals (weddings, funerals, naming ceremonies), moral frameworks for decision-making, and a sense of belonging and purpose. As these institutions decline, younger people must find alternative sources for all of it.

The ideological vacuum

Religion provided comprehensive worldviews — answers to “Why do we exist?” “What is morally right?” “How should we treat others?” Secular society offers fragments of answers through various ideologies but no unified framework. Some young people seek replacement frameworks (Buddhism, yoga spirituality, progressive politics); others accept a more fragmented, individualised approach to meaning-making.

The social capital question

Religious communities build social capital — dense networks of mutual obligation and support. As these communities decline, so does the practical support network younger people can rely on. This may be contributing to the rising rates of mental-health challenges, isolation, and loneliness among young people.

The colonialism angle

For communities with histories of colonisation — Latinos, Filipinos, Black Americans, Native Americans — religious disaffiliation carries additional significance. From respondents:

“Christianity is a cult imposed by white imperialism to keep Black people subjugated and ignore their own beliefs.” — “Islam, Christianity isn’t for Black folks; spirituality and nature is what we need to get back to.”

Rejecting the colonially-imposed religion becomes an act of decolonisation. The paradox: while intellectually sound, the practical and emotional moorings the religion provided — community, moral framework, cultural continuity — are difficult to replace. Young people leaving colonial religions often struggle to build alternative communities that provide equivalent support.

Section 10What the Future Likely Holds

Projected trajectory (next 20–30 years)

  • Latino communities — continued decline in Catholic affiliation among US-born generations; evangelical growth may plateau or reverse; emergence of “cultural Catholicism” without active practice; possible Catholic Church downsizing of US institutional presence.
  • Filipino communities — continued disaffiliation among second- and third-generation Filipino Americans; maintenance of Catholicism among recent immigrants; possible development of more secular Filipino community organisations.
  • Asian American communities — near-total collapse of ethnic churches as Baby Boomer and Gen X members age; possible emergence of smaller, progressive, English-language Asian American religious communities; shift toward secular cultural organisations for community-building.
  • Black communities — continued exodus from traditional Black churches; modest growth in LGBTQ+-affirming and progressive Black churches; possible emergence of secular Black community organisations focused on social justice and mutual aid; gradual decoupling of Black identity from Christian identity.

Broader societal implications

  • Institutional vacuum. As churches decline, who provides community, crisis support and meaning-making? Currently unanswered.
  • Political realignment. Reduced religious affiliation may weaken conservative voting blocs, but it’s unclear what moral and ethical frameworks will replace religious influence on policy.
  • Meaning crisis. Societies with low religiosity (Western Europe, parts of East Asia) have developed robust secular frameworks. American society is still figuring this out.
  • Potential religious backlash. As disaffiliation accelerates, remaining religious communities may become more militant and reactionary, deepening polarisation.

Section 11Conclusion — Understanding the Moment

The data from these communities reveals something larger than a decline in church attendance. It documents a fundamental shift in how younger people construct meaning, community and identity.

Key takeaways

  • This is not primarily a faith crisis but an institutional crisis. Young people haven’t necessarily rejected God; they’ve rejected churches that embody values opposed to what they’ve learned about justice, equality and human dignity.
  • Education and information access are accelerants. When people have access to alternative frameworks, historical knowledge and diverse communities, inherited religious traditions become optional rather than mandatory.
  • The hypocrisy gap is fatal. Institutions that preach one set of values while practising another cannot retain members who have been trained to notice and question inconsistencies.
  • Adaptability is essential for survival. Churches that modernise theology and practices retain younger members better than those prioritising tradition preservation.
  • This is a global phenomenon, not a local problem. The same patterns appear across religions, denominations, geographies and cultures — the issue is structural rather than specific to any one community.
  • The practical costs are real. As religious communities decline, alternative institutions providing community support, moral frameworks and social capital have not yet emerged to fill the void.

The disaffiliation trend documented across Latino, Filipino, Asian American and Black communities represents one of the most significant social changes of the early 21st century — rivalling in importance the 1960s secularisation of Western Europe. Its full implications, both positive and negative, will take decades to fully understand.
